<Section id="context" n="01" title="Context" lede="A convenience-store chain was replacing its monolithic point-of-sale system with microservices.">

The services talk over MQTT. I was a senior developer on a team of about 15, consulting through Momentum3, and I owned money orders end to end, from business rules to printing on real store hardware.

<Parts items={[
  ['Conductor', 'Coordinates a sale across the other services.'],

]} />

</Section>

<Section id="problem" n="02" title="Problem" lede="Printing is slow and can fail partway, and the old flow had no clean way back.">

Totaling the transaction waited on the printer, so the register sat blocked during every print. When a print failed, the failure tangled with the normal path. In payments, a failure can't leave the customer charged for a document they never got.

</Section>

<Section id="options" n="03" title="Options" lede="I weighed keeping printing inside the transaction against splitting the flow into steps that each recover on their own.">

<Options>
  <Option label="Option A" verdict="Rejected" title="Keep printing in the transaction">Simplest to change. The register still blocks while it prints, and every failure still runs through the happy path.</Option>
  <Option label="Option B" chosen title="Run it as a saga">Separate steps, each with its own recovery. More moving parts, but a failed print has one clear place to go.</Option>
</Options>

</Section>

<Section id="decision" n="04" title="Decision" lede="I redesigned the flow in the conductor service as a saga.">

1. Totaling continues while printing runs asynchronously.
2. The print timeout scales with the number of documents, so a large order isn't cut off early.
3. Failed prints go down their own path and trigger an automatic refund transaction.
4. A low-document alert warns the store before the printer runs out.

<Callout label="How I proved it" title="Tests for every path the transaction could take">
I planned tests for all prints failing (refund, no details recorded), all succeeding, and a mix of two successes and one failure. Others covered the timeout scaling, the timeout expiring with the right exception, and the low-document alert. Then I verified it on real money-order hardware in the store lab.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="outcome" n="05" title="Outcome" lede="A failed print now refunds the customer automatically, in the same flow.">

Money orders were built and verified in the store lab with configurable limits, no refunds after printing, employee-ID verification, and fees as sub-line items.

The whole team, including the product owner, signed off on it in a demo. I picked up the saga pattern from the fuel service, and after money orders, the boot-up and checkout flows used it too.

</Section>
