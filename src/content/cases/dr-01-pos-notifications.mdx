<Section id="context" n="01" title="Context" lede="A convenience-store chain was replacing its monolithic point-of-sale system with microservices.">

The services talk over MQTT, using both pub/sub and RPC topics. I was a senior developer on a team of about 15, consulting through Momentum3. The system was greenfield, and I designed its notification service.

<Parts items={[
  ['Conductor', 'Coordinates a sale and sends notifications to the UI.'],
  ['Transaction', 'Owns the sale, and so where it lives.'],
  ['Hardware', 'Drives the store hardware.'],
  ['UI', 'Shows the notification and collects the cashier\'s answer.'],
]} />

</Section>

<Section id="problem" n="02" title="Problem" lede="Notifications travel between the conductor and the UI, and they don't all behave the same way.">

Some events only need a heads-up. Others must stop a single register, and a few must stop the whole POS until someone answers. Those blocking ones need a reply from the cashier, so the sender has to wait for it. The informational ones don't.

</Section>

<Section id="options" n="03" title="Options" lede="I presented two approaches to the tech leads, split by how a notification moves over MQTT.">

<Options>
  <Option label="Option A" verdict="Combined" title="Everything over pub/sub">Fire and forget. Simple and fast for informational messages, but nothing waits for the cashier's answer.</Option>
  <Option label="Option B" verdict="Combined" title="Everything over RPC">Every notification waits for a reply. Right for blocking messages, but heavy for a simple heads-up.</Option>
</Options>

</Section>

<Section id="decision" n="04" title="Decision" lede="The team adopted a hybrid that separates what a notification does from how it looks.">

1. **Severity picks the transport.** Blocking notifications (block one register, block the whole POS) go over RPC and wait for an answer. Informational ones go over pub/sub.
2. **Category only sets the look,** such as color and position on screen.
3. Because the two are separate, a notification's look and its behavior can change without touching each other.

<Callout label="What changed later" title="Position moved from the UI to the backend">
The plan was for the UI to set where a notification appears. It moved to the backend, because the backend owns where the transaction lives, and that decides where the notification should show.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="outcome" n="05" title="Outcome" lede="The design held up as the POS grew.">

Adding new notification types stayed easy. The same service later carried hardware flows I built, such as POS reboot and stop POS, which use a notification for verification instead of a password.

</Section>
