Redesigned money-order printing so a failed print refunds the customer automatically

TL;DR 10 min read
Problem

A money order is paid for before it prints. A failed print left the customer charged with nothing in hand.

Decision

Treat printing as one step in a transaction, with the refund as its compensating action.

Outcome

A failed print now refunds the customer automatically, with no manual step.

Role
Lead developer
Stack
C#, .NET, REST APIs
On this page
01

Context

Money orders are sold at the register and printed on a separate printer at the counter.

One sale touches three systems, and each can fail on its own. The register takes the payment, the provider's API issues the money order and its serial number, and the printer puts that serial on paper.

Register
Point-of-sale app in C# and .NET. Takes the payment and runs the sale.
Provider API
Issues the money order and its serial number. Requires payment first.
Printer
Local device on the counter. Reports printed, failed, or nothing.
02

Problem

The old flow took the payment and issued the serial before printing, with no plan for a print that failed.

When the printer jammed or dropped offline mid-sale, the customer had paid and the serial was live, but nothing came out. The clerk called support, a manager voided the serial in the provider's portal, and someone refunded the customer by hand. Customers waited at the counter or came back later, and a missed void left a live serial with no paper behind it.

Failure modes
Failed
The printer reports a jam or misfeed.
Offline
The printer can't be reached when the sale starts.
Unknown
The print was sent and no status came back.
03

Options

I compared four approaches against one rule: the customer never leaves charged with nothing in hand.

Option AKept, not enough
Retry the print

Clears a jam that fixes itself. It doesn't help when the printer stays down, and the customer is charged the whole time.

Option BNot possible
Print before taking payment

The provider only issues a serial after payment, so there is nothing to print yet.

Option CRejected
Keep the manual process, add a checklist

Cheapest to ship. The customer still waits, and the void is still easy to miss.

Option DChosen
Undo each step when printing fails

Treat the sale as a sequence of steps, each with an undo. If printing fails, void the serial, then refund the payment.

04

Decision

Each sale runs as a short saga: the register records every step, and every step after payment has an undo.

Before it calls anything, the register writes the sale to a local ledger and checks that the printer is ready, which removes the offline case before any money moves. Every call after that carries the sale's idempotency key, so a retry after a timeout can't issue a second serial or a second refund.

Printed Started→ Paid→ Issued→ Printed→ Done
Failed Started→ Paid→ Issued→ Voided→ Refunded
No answer Started→ Paid→ Issued→ Held→ Clerk decides

The failure branch (lines 14–16) undoes the steps in reverse order. The serial is voided before the payment is refunded, so a refund never goes out for a money order that is still valid.

Edge case

No answer from the printer

If the printer never reports back, the register can't tell whether paper came out. An automatic refund could hand out a valid money order for free, so the sale is held and the clerk answers one question on screen: “Did a money order print?” The answer is saved with the sale.

05

Outcome

A failed print now ends at the counter, with the customer refunded in the same transaction.

Before
  1. 1Clerk calls support.
  2. 2A manager voids the serial in the provider's portal.
  3. 3Someone refunds the customer by hand.
After
  1. 1The register voids the serial.
  2. 2The register refunds the payment.
  3. 3The receipt shows the refund and the reason.

The ledger also gives support a record of every failed print, with the printer's last reported status.

06

What I'd tell an interviewer

  1. 1

    Most of the work was deciding what “failed” means.

    The refund itself is a few lines of code. The unknown print status took most of the design time, and it's the part I'd walk through on a whiteboard.

  2. 2

    Idempotency keys mattered more than the saga.

    Double charges come from retries and timeouts. One key per sale, passed to every call, closed that off.

  3. 3

    I'd design the ledger first next time.

    It started as a debugging log and became what support relies on. Designing it up front would have saved rework.
